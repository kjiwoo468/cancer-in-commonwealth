Informational tool

Cancer risk calculator

Answer a short set of questions about your habits, history, and environment to see a simplified, educational snapshot of how those factors combine — modeled on the same basic approach used by published cancer risk tools.

This is not a diagnostic tool

This calculator is for informational and educational purposes only. It cannot tell you whether you have cancer, whether you will develop it, or your exact numerical chance of getting it — no online tool can do that reliably. It does not replace a doctor, a screening test, or medical advice. If you have questions about your own health, talk to a parent or guardian, a school nurse, or a health care provider.

Where this approach comes from

Most public cancer-risk tools don't try to output an exact percentage chance of getting cancer — the science isn't precise enough for that at an individual level. Instead, two well-known tools use a relative-risk approach: they compare a person's combination of factors to the average person of the same age, and report whether that combination is below, about, or above average.

  • The Harvard Cancer Risk Index, developed by researchers at the Harvard Center for Cancer Prevention and still used today as Your Disease Risk (Siteman Cancer Center, Washington University in St. Louis), assigns each risk factor a point value based on how strongly research links it to cancer, sums those points, and compares the total to a population average.
  • My CancerIQ, built by Cancer Care Ontario / Ontario Health, works the same basic way: each factor gets a number showing how much it raises or lowers risk, and the combined score is compared to the average person of the same age and sex — then sorted into a small number of risk bands rather than a precise percentage.

How this calculator scores your answers

This tool uses a simplified version of the same idea. Every answer starts at a neutral 0 points. Each answer then adds or subtracts points based on how strongly published research links that factor to cancer risk, using the same weak / moderate / strong / very strong scale that the Your Disease Risk reference table itself publishes:

±1 — weak association ±2 — moderate association ±3 — strong association ±4 — very strong association

All of your points are added together into one total, and that total places you into one of five bands: well below average, below average, about average, above average, or well above average — always relative to other people, never a specific percentage or prediction.

What this calculator leaves out (on purpose)

  • It does not ask about weight/BMI. Adult weight cutoffs used in research don't translate cleanly to teenagers, whose bodies are still developing.
  • It does not ask about sex-specific factors (like reproductive or hormonal history) so that it stays relevant to every student answering it.
  • It only covers some of the many known risk factors, and it can't account for genetic testing, specific medical conditions, or the interactions researchers are still studying.
  • It reports one combined score across cancer in general — real clinical tools usually calculate risk separately for each specific cancer type.
